L7016: 1.2. Introduction to Limit State
L7017: 1.  Analysis and Design
L7018: • Analysis refers to the determination of the axial forces, shear forces, bending moments, torsional moments etc acting
L7019: on different members of a structure due to the applied loads and load combinations
L7020: • Design involves the selection of shape and size of the member and connection details of various members (beam to
L7021: beam, beam to column, column to foundation etc) to resist all forces and moments determined in the analysis safely
L7022: and economically
L7023: 2.  Design requirements of steel structure
L7024: • To fit for their purpose (Should sustain all anticipate loads expected on it and Should withstand all deformations during
L7025: and after construction)
L7026: • Should be safe should be economical and durable
L7027: 3. Uncertainties in design
L7028: The uncertainties affecting the safety of a structure are due to
L7029: • Uncertainty about loading (unfavorable deviation of the load from its characteristic value. Inaccurate assessment of
L7030: the load, improper assessment of load effect etc)
L7031: • Uncertainty about material strength (Unfavorable deviation of  material strength from its characteristic value)
L7032: • Uncertainty about structural dimensions (variation membe r sizes) due to fabrication tolerances.
L7033: • Uncertainty in the calculation of strength of the member
L7034: 4. Limit States
L7035: • Its  acceptable limit for the safety and serviceability requirement before failure occurs is called limit s tate.
L7036: • Limit states are basically two categories, strength  and serviceability.
L7037: • It is basically statistical method have been used for determination of load and material properties with small
L7038: probability of structure reaching limit state of st rength and serviceability.
L7039: 5.  Types of limit states

L7151: BOLTED CONNECTIONS

L7153: 2.1. Introduction
L7154: • A bolt may defined as metal pin with head at one end as shank threaded at other end to receive a nut. Steel washers are
L7155: usually provided under bolt as well as under the nut to distribute the clamping pressure on the bolted member and to
L7156: prevent the threaded portion of the bolt from bearing on the connected pieces.
L7157: • Bolts can be used for making end connection in tension and compression members. They can also be used to hold
L7158: column bases in position.

L7160: 2.2. Classification of Bolted Connection
L7161: The bolted connections are classified based on geometry and loading conditions into three types namely
L7162: (a) Classification based on the type of resultant force transferred:
L7163: • Concentric connections (force transfer in tension and Compression member),
L7164: • Eccentric connections (in reaction transferring brackets) or moment resisting connections (in beam to column
L7165: connections in frames).


L7168: ===== Page 216 / 947 =====

L7173: (b) Classification based on the type of force experienced by the bolts:
L7174: • Shear connections
L7175: • Tension connections and
L7176: • Combined shear and tension Connections

L7178: (c) Classification based on force transfer mechanism by bolts:
L7179: • Bearing type (bolts bear against the holes to transfer the force)
L7180: • Friction type (force transfer between the plates due to the clamping force generated by the pre-tensioning of the bolts).

L7182: 2.3.  Types of Bolts
L7183: They are several types of bolts used to connect structural members. Some of them are listed below:
L7184: (a) Black Bolts or unfinished bolts
L7185: • Black bolts are referred to as ordinary, rough or common bolts. They are least expensive bolts and are made of low
L7186: carbon steels (mild steel) with square or hexagonal head. the diameter of the hole is about 1.5 to 2.0 mm larger than
L7187: the bolt diameter for ease in fitting.
L7188: • They are primarily used in light structures under static loads such as small trusses, purlins, bracings. They are also used
L7189: as temporary fasteners during erection where HSFG bolts or welding are used as permanent fasteners.

L7196: • These bolts are not recommended for connections subjected to impact load, vibration and fatigue.
L7197: • For bolt of a property class 4.6 represents the ultimate tensile strength is 400 N/mm2 and yield strength is 0.6 times
L7198: 400 which is 240 N/mm2.
L7199: • Ordinary bolted joints, the force transfer through interlocking and bearing of bolts and joint is called bearing type joint.
L7200: (b) High Strength Friction Grip (HSFG) Bolts.
L7201: • High strength friction grip bolts are made from bars of medium carbon heat treated steel (high tensile steel). The bolt
L7202: property class 10.9S and 12.9S are commonly used in steel connections.
L7203: • The HSFG bolts are available in sizes from 16mm to 36mm and are designated as M16, M20, M24 and M30.
L7204: • These bolts tightened (by torque wrenches) until they have very high tensile stresses, so that connected parts are
L7205: clamped tightly together between the bolt head and nut, this permits load to be transferred
L7206: • primarily by friction not by shear.
L7207: • These bolts are most suitable for bridges where the stress reversal may occur or slippage is undesirable also for seismic
L7208: loading and for fatigue load are ideal.
L7209: • High strength bolts have replaced rivets and black bolts are being used in structures, high raised building, bridges etc
L7210: 2.4. Types of bolted joints
L7211: They are two types of bolted joints subjected to axial force (the loads are assumed to pass through the C.G of the group of
L7212: bolts)
L7213: (a) Lap joint
L7214: • The two members to be connected are overlapped and connected together such a joint is called lap joint.
L7215: • The load in a lap joint has eccentricity, as the centre of gravity of load in one member and centre of gravity of load in
L7216: second member are not in a same line, therefore a couple formed which causes undesirable bending.

L7218: (b) Butt joint
L7219: • Two members to be connected placed end to end. Additional cover plate/plates are provided on either one or both sides,
L7220: called cover plates are placed and connected to main plate.
L7221: • If cover plate is provided on one side it is called single cover butt joint.
L7222: • If cover plate is provided on both sides of main plate, it is called a double cover butt joint.
L7223: • Double cover butt joint, eccentricity of a force doesn't exist and hence bending is eliminated, where as it exists in the
L7224: case of lap joint.

L7442: WELDED CONNECTIONS

L7444: 3.1. Introduction
L7445: 1. Welding and Welded connections
L7446: • Welding is the process of joining two pieces of metal by creating a strong metallurgic bond between them by heating
L7447: (fusion) or pressure or both. It is distinguished from other forms of mechanical connections, such as riveting or bolting,
L7448: which are formed by friction or mechanical connections, such as riveting or bolting, which are formed by friction or
L7449: mechanical interlocking.
L7450: • For steel structures metal or electric welding is generally used.
L7451: 2. Advantages and disadvantages of welded connections
L7452: Welding offers many advantages over bolting and riveting.
L7453: • Welding enables direct transfer of stress between members eliminating gusset and splice plates necessary for bolted
L7454: structures. Hence, the weight of the joint is minimum.
L7455: • In the case of tension members, the absence of holes improves the strength and efficiency of the section.
L7456: • It involves less fabrication cost compared to other methods due to handling of fewer parts and elimination of operations
L7457: like drilling, punching etc. and consequently less labour leading to economy.
L7458: • Welding offers air tight and water tight joining and hence is ideal for oil storage tanks, ships etc.
L7459: • Welded structures also have a neat appearance
L7460: • Welded structures arc more rigid compared to structures with riveted and bolted connections.
L7461: • A truly continuous structure is formed by the process of fusing the members together.
L7462: • Generally welded joints are as strong or stronger than the base metal, thereby placing no restriction on the joints.
L7463: • Stress concentration effect is also considerably less in a welded connection.
L7464: • Some of the disadvantages of welding are that it requires skilled manpower for welding as well as inspection.
L7465: • Also, non-destructive evaluation may have to be carried out to detect defects in welds.
L7466: • Welding in the field may be difficult due to the location or environment. Welded joints are highly prone to cracking
L7467: under fatigue loading.
L7468: • Large residual stresses and distortion are developed in welded connections.

L7640: 4.1. Eccentric Bolted Connection
L7641: (1) Introduction
L7642: Connections become complex when they have to transmit axial and shear forces in addition to bending moments or twisting
L7643: moments, between structural members oriented in different directions.
L7644: (2) Concentric load
L7645: A load is said to be concentric load, when its line of action passes through centre of gravity of bolt group or rivet group
L7646: or weld group
L7647: (3) Eccentric load
L7648: A load is called eccentric load when its line of action does not pass through centre of gravity of bolt or rivet or weld
L7649: group. Because of eccentricity additional moment is induced in the joint.
L7650: (4) Beam to Column Connections
L7651: Beam to column connections can be classified as simple, semi-rigid and rigid depending on the amount of moment
L7652: transfer taking place between the beam to the column.
L7653: • Simple connections are assumed to transfer only shear at some nominal eccentricity. Therefore such connections can
L7654: be used only in non-sway frames where the lateral loads are resisted by some alternative arrangement such as bracings
L7655: or shear walls.
L7656: • Rigid connections transfer significant moments
L7657: to the columns and are assumed to undergo
L7658: negligible deformations. Rigid connections are
L7659: necessary in sway frames for stability and also
L7660: contribute in resisting lateral loads.
L7661: • A third type connection which resists end
L7662: moments as well as permits relative rotation

L7863: TENSION MEMBERS

L7865: 5.1. Introduction
L7866: Tension members are linear members in which axial forces act so as to elongate (stretch) the member. A rope, for example, is
L7867: a tension member. Tension members carry loads most efficiently, since the entire cross section is subjected to uniform stress .
L7868: Unlike compression members, they do not fail by buckling. Ties of trusses, suspenders of cable stayed and suspension bridges,
L7869: suspenders of buildings systems hung from a central core (such buildings are used in earthquake prone zones as a way of
L7870: minimising inertia forces on the structure), and sag rods of roof purlins are other examples of tension members.
L7871: Tension members are also encountered as bracings used for the lateral load resistance. In X type bracings the member which is
L7872: under tension, due to lateral load acting in one direction, undergoes compressive force, when the direction of the lateral load is
L7873: changed and vice versa. Hence, such members may have to be designed to resist tensile and compressive forces.


L7876: 5.2. Types of Cross-sections
L7877: The tension members can have a variety of cross sections. The single angle and double angle sections are used in light roof
L7878: trusses as in industrial buildings. The tension members in bridge trusses are made of channels or I sections, acting individually
L7879: or built-up. The circular rods are used in bracings designed to resist loads in tension only. They buckle at very low compression
L7880: and are not considered effective. Steel wire ropes are used as suspenders in the cable suspended bridges and as main stays in
L7881: the cable-stayed bridges.

L8075: 6.1. Introduction
L8076: • A compression member is a structural member which is subjected to two equal opposite compressive forces applied at its
L8077: ends. There are may types of compression members, the column being the best known. Top chords of trusses, bracing
L8078: members, boom is another principle compression member in a crane and compression flanges of built up beams and
L8079: rolled beams are all examples of compression elements. Columns are usually though of a as straight vertical members
L8080: whose lengths are considerably greater than their cross-sectional dimensions.
L8081: • An initially straight strut or column, compressed by gradually increasing equal and opposite axial forces at the ends is
L8082: considered first. Columns and struts are termed “long” or “short” depending on their proneness to buckling. If the strut is
L8083: “short”, the applied forces will cause a compressive strain, which results in the shortening of the strut in the direction of
L8084: the applied forces. Under incremental loading, this shortening continues until the column "squashes". However, if the
L8085: strut is “long” , similar axial shortening is observed only at the initial stages of incremental loading. Therefore, as the
L8086: applied forc es are increased in magnitude, the strut becomes “ unstable” and develops a deformation in a direction
L8087: normal to the loading axis. The strut is in a “buckled” state.
L8088: • Bucking (mainly in members subjected to compressive forces) behaviour is thus characterized by deformations
L8089: developed in a direction (or plane) normal to that of the loading that produces it.
L8090: 6.2. General failures in axially loaded columns
L8091: • Very short columns subjected to axial compression fail by yielding or crushing.
L8092: • Very long columns fail by elastic buckling in the Euler mode.
L8093: • Intermediate columns generally fail by inelastic buckling.
