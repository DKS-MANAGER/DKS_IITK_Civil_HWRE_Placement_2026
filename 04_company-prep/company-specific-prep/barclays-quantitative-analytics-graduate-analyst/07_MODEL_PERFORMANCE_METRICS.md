A model can have **superb discrimination** (it ranks high-risk borrowers higher than low-risk borrowers) while simultaneously having **terrible calibration** (e.g., predicting an average default rate of 1.0% when the macro-environment causes an actual default rate of 4.5%). Conversely, a model can be well-calibrated in aggregate while losing discriminatory power (collapsing score separation).

### 1. Discrimination (Rank-Ordering) Metrics

Discrimination measures the model's capacity to assign higher default scores (or probabilities) to borrowers who subsequently default ($Y = 1$) than to borrowers who do not default ($Y = 0$), regardless of the absolute scale of the predicted probabilities.

---

#### 1.1 Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) & Area Under the Curve (AUC)

Let the continuous score or predicted probability generated by the model be denoted as $S \in \mathbb{R}$. Let $Y \in \{0, 1\}$ represent the observed outcome over the performance window (typically 12 months), where $Y = 1$ denotes a "Bad" (default) and $Y = 0$ denotes a "Good" (non-default).

Given a decision threshold $c$, any account with $S \ge c$ is classified as predicted default. We define:
- **True Positive Rate (Sensitivity / Hit Rate):**
  $$\text{TPR}(c) = P(S \ge c \mid Y = 1) = 1 - F_{\text{bad}}(c)$$
- **False Positive Rate (1 - Specificity / Fall-out):**
  $$\text{FPR}(c) = P(S \ge c \mid Y = 0) = 1 - F_{\text{good}}(c)$$

where $F_{\text{bad}}(c)$ and $F_{\text{good}}(c)$ are the cumulative distribution functions of the score among bads and goods, respectively.

The **Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC)** curve plots $\text{TPR}(c)$ on the vertical axis against $\text{FPR}(c)$ on the horizontal axis as the threshold $c$ varies continuously from $+\infty$ to $-\infty$.

##### Mathematical Equivalence to the Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney U Statistic
The Area Under the ROC Curve ($\text{AUC}$) is mathematically equivalent to the probability that a randomly chosen bad account has a higher predicted risk score than a randomly chosen good account:

$$\text{AUC} = \int_{0}^{1} \text{TPR}(\text{FPR}^{-1}(u)) \, du = P(S_{\text{bad}} > S_{\text{good}}) + \frac{1}{2} P(S_{\text{bad}} = S_{\text{good}})$$

Given a sample of $N_1$ bad accounts with scores $\{s_{1, 1}, s_{1, 2}, \dots, s_{1, N_1}\}$ and $N_0$ good accounts with scores $\{s_{0, 1}, s_{0, 2}, \dots, s_{0, N_0}\}$:

$$\text{AUC} = \frac{1}{N_1 N_0} \sum_{i=1}^{N_1} \sum_{j=1}^{N_0} I(s_{1, i}, s_{0, j})$$

where the indicator function is:
$$I(s_{1, i}, s_{0, j}) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } s_{1, i} > s_{0, j} \\ 0.5 & \text{if } s_{1, i} = s_{0, j} \\ 0 & \text{if } s_{1, i} < s_{0, j} \end{cases}$$

Using the sum of ranks of the bad accounts $R_1$ in the pooled ranking of all $N = N_0 + N_1$ accounts:
$$\text{AUC} = \frac{R_1 - \frac{N_1(N_1 + 1)}{2}}{N_1 N_0}$$

##### Standard Error and Confidence Interval (Hanley-McNeil Formula)
$$\text{SE}(\text{AUC}) = \sqrt{\frac{\text{AUC}(1 - \text{AUC}) + (N_1 - 1)(Q_1 - \text{AUC}^2) + (N_0 - 1)(Q_2 - \text{AUC}^2)}{N_1 N_0}}$$
where $Q_1 = \frac{\text{AUC}}{2 - \text{AUC}}$ and $Q_2 = \frac{2 \times \text{AUC}^2}{1 + \text{AUC}}$.

#### 1.2 Gini Coefficient & Cumulative Accuracy Profile (CAP / Lorenz)

In credit risk modeling, practitioners frequently express discrimination using the **Gini coefficient** (also known as Somers' $D$ or the Accuracy Ratio $AR$ from the Cumulative Accuracy Profile curve).

##### Mathematical Derivation from AUC
The Gini coefficient scales the AUC so that a random model ($\text{AUC} = 0.50$) corresponds to a Gini of $0$, and a theoretically perfect model ($\text{AUC} = 1.00$) corresponds to a Gini of $1.00$ ($100\%$):

$$\text{Gini} = 2 \times \text{AUC} - 1 = \frac{A}{A + B}$$

where $A$ is the area between the model's ROC curve and the diagonal 45-degree line of no discrimination, and $A + B = 0.50$ is the total area of the upper triangle.

#### 1.3 Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) Statistic

The **Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic** measures the maximum vertical distance between the cumulative distribution function of Bads ($F_{\text{bad}}$) and the cumulative distribution function of Goods ($F_{\text{good}}$) across all possible score cutoffs:

$$\text{KS} = \max_{s} \left| F_{\text{bad}}(s) - F_{\text{good}}(s) \right|$$

In scorecard monitoring, accounts are grouped into 10 score deciles (ranked from highest risk to lowest risk, or vice versa). For each decile $k \in \{1, 2, \dots, 10\}$:
$$\text{KS} = \max_{k=1}^{10} \left| \text{CumBad}\%_k - \text{CumGood}\%_k \right|$$

##### Typical Industry & Barclays Benchmark:
- $\text{KS} > 40\%$: Strong discriminatory power.
- $30\% \le \text{KS} \le 40\%$: Acceptable discriminatory power.
- $20\% \le \text{KS} < 30\%$: Weak performance; trigger Amber monitoring flag.
- $\text{KS} < 20\%$: Model failure; trigger Red breach, investigate portfolio shift.

### 2. Calibration (Accuracy) Metrics

Calibration measures whether the **magnitude** of the predicted default probabilities matches the **actual observed default frequency**. A model can rank-order borrowers perfectly (Gini = 1.0) but predict $PD = 0.01\%$ when the real default rate is $5.0\%$, creating catastrophic under-provisioning under IFRS 9 and Basel IRB.

---

#### 2.1 Observed vs. Expected ($O/E$) Ratio

For each rating grade or score bucket $g \in \{1, 2, \dots, G\}$ over a 12-month monitoring outcome window:

$$\text{Expected Defaults } (E_g) = \sum_{i \in \text{Grade } g} PD_i$$
$$\text{Observed Defaults } (O_g) = \sum_{i \in \text{Grade } g} Y_i$$
$$\text{Calibration Ratio } (O/E)_g = \frac{O_g}{E_g} = \frac{\bar{Y}_g}{\overline{PD}_g}$$

- **$O/E \approx 1.0$**: Well-calibrated rating grade.
- **$O/E > 1.0$**: Under-prediction of risk (aggressive model, potential capital/provision shortfall).
- **$O/E < 1.0$**: Over-prediction of risk (conservative model, capital inefficiency).

##### One-Sided and Two-Sided Binomial Hypothesis Test for $O/E$
Under the null hypothesis $H_0: p = PD_g$, the observed number of defaults $O_g$ follows a Binomial distribution $O_g \sim \text{Binomial}(N_g, PD_g)$.
For large $N_g$, the normal approximation yields the test statistic:

$$Z_g = \frac{O_g - N_g \cdot PD_g}{\sqrt{N_g \cdot PD_g (1 - PD_g)}} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$$

Traffic-light thresholds in Barclays Model Monitoring:
- **Green**: $|Z_g| < 1.96$ ($p\text{-value} > 0.05$, two-tailed)
- **Amber**: $1.96 \le |Z_g| < 2.58$ ($0.01 < p\text{-value} \le 0.05$)
- **Red**: $|Z_g| \ge 2.58$ ($p\text{-value} \le 0.01$)

---

#### 2.2 Hosmer-Lemeshow Goodness-of-Fit Test

The Hosmer-Lemeshow (HL) test assesses calibration across the entire portfolio by partitioning accounts into $G$ groups (typically $G = 10$ deciles of predicted probability) and testing the difference between observed and expected frequencies:

$$H = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \left[ \frac{(O_g - N_g \bar{\pi}_g)^2}{N_g \bar{\pi}_g} + \frac{((N_g - O_g) - N_g (1 - \bar{\pi}_g))^2}{N_g (1 - \bar{\pi}_g)} \right] = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \frac{(O_g - N_g \bar{\pi}_g)^2}{N_g \bar{\pi}_g (1 - \bar{\pi}_g)}$$

where:
- $N_g$ is the total number of accounts in group $g$.
- $O_g$ is the observed number of defaults in group $g$.
- $\bar{\pi}_g = \frac{1}{N_g} \sum_{i \in g} \hat{p}_i$ is the average predicted probability in group $g$.

Under the null hypothesis $H_0$ that the model is correctly calibrated:
$$H \sim \chi^2_{G - 2} \quad (\text{degrees of freedom } df = 10 - 2 = 8 \text{ for 10 deciles})$$

> **Critical QA Interview Insight on Hosmer-Lemeshow:**  
> In massive retail credit portfolios ($N > 200,000$ accounts), the standard Hosmer-Lemeshow test almost **always rejects the null hypothesis** ($p < 0.001$) even when the economic calibration error is trivial ($< 0.1\%$). This occurs because the test statistic scales directly with $N$. In Barclays Risk QA, analysts supplement the HL test with effect-size measures such as Root Mean Squared Calibration Error (RMSCE) or traffic-light binomial tests per rating grade.

---

#### 2.3 Calibration-in-the-Large (CITL) & Calibration Slope

Calibration can be formally evaluated using a logistic recalibration regression:

$$\text{logit}(P(Y_i = 1)) = \alpha + \beta \cdot \text{logit}(\hat{p}_i)$$

where $\text{logit}(p) = \ln\left(\frac{p}{1 - p}\right)$.

1. **Calibration-in-the-Large (CITL, $\alpha$ with $\beta$ fixed to 1):**
   $$\text{logit}(P(Y_i = 1)) = \alpha + \text{offset}(\text{logit}(\hat{p}_i))$$
   - Tests whether predicted probabilities are systematically too high or too low overall.
   - Ideal value: $\alpha = 0$.
   - $\alpha > 0$: Portfolio default rate is higher than predicted (systematic underestimation).
   - $\alpha < 0$: Portfolio default rate is lower than predicted (systematic overestimation).

2. **Calibration Slope ($\beta$ estimated freely):**
   - Tests whether predicted probabilities are too extreme or too moderate.
   - Ideal value: $\beta = 1.0$.
   - $\beta < 1.0$: Over-fitting; predicted probabilities are too extreme (high scores are too high, low scores are too low). Common in machine learning models tested on out-of-time datasets.
   - $\beta > 1.0$: Under-fitting; predicted probabilities do not vary enough across risk classes.

---

#### 2.4 Brier Score & Murphy Decomposition

The **Brier Score** measures overall accuracy as the mean squared error of predicted probabilities:

$$\text{BS} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (Y_i - \hat{p}_i)^2 \quad \in [0, 1]$$

A lower Brier score indicates superior accuracy. The Murphy Decomposition breaks the Brier score into three interpretable components across $G$ rating grades:

$$\text{BS} = \text{Reliability} - \text{Resolution} + \text{Uncertainty}$$

$$\text{Reliability} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \frac{N_g}{N} (\bar{y}_g - \bar{\pi}_g)^2 \quad \text{(Calibration penalty, ideal = 0)}$$
$$\text{Resolution} = \sum_{g=1}^{G} \frac{N_g}{N} (\bar{y}_g - \bar{y})^2 \quad \text{(Discrimination reward, higher is better)}$$
$$\text{Uncertainty} = \bar{y}(1 - \bar{y}) \quad \text{(Intrinsic portfolio variance, independent of model)}$$

### 3. Stability Metrics

Stability metrics quantify whether the **distribution of borrower characteristics** or the **distribution of model scores** has shifted between the baseline development sample (or prior quarter) and the current monitoring period.

---

#### 3.1 Population Stability Index (PSI)

The **Population Stability Index (PSI)** is the industry-standard measure for detecting score distribution shifts.

##### Derivation from Symmetrized Kullback-Leibler (KL) Divergence
Let $P = \{E_1, E_2, \dots, E_k\}$ represent the expected (development or baseline) probability distribution across $k$ score buckets, where $\sum_{i=1}^k E_i = 1.0$.
Let $Q = \{A_1, A_2, \dots, A_k\}$ represent the actual (current monitoring period) probability distribution, where $\sum_{i=1}^k A_i = 1.0$.

The Kullback-Leibler divergence from $P$ to $Q$ is:
$$D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} A_i \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right)$$

Because $D_{\text{KL}}$ is asymmetric ($D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P) \ne D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q)$), the Population Stability Index is defined as the symmetrized KL divergence (also known as Jeffreys divergence):

$$\text{PSI} = D_{\text{KL}}(Q \parallel P) + D_{\text{KL}}(P \parallel Q) = \sum_{i=1}^{k} A_i \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right) + \sum_{i=1}^{k} E_i \ln\left(\frac{E_i}{A_i}\right)$$

Combining terms:
$$\text{PSI} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} \left[ A_i \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right) - E_i \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right) \right] = \sum_{i=1}^{k} (A_i - E_i) \times \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right)$$

##### Proof of Non-Negativity:
For any $x > 0$, the inequality $(x - 1) \ln(x) \ge 0$ holds with equality if and only if $x = 1$.
Setting $x = \frac{A_i}{E_i}$:
$$(A_i - E_i) \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right) = E_i \left(\frac{A_i}{E_i} - 1\right) \ln\left(\frac{A_i}{E_i}\right) \ge 0$$
Since $E_i > 0$, every individual bucket contribution is strictly non-negative. Therefore, $\text{PSI} \ge 0$, with $\text{PSI} = 0$ if and only if $A_i = E_i$ for all $i$.

##### Standard Regulatory & Barclays Thresholds:
| PSI Value | Traffic Light | Interpretation & Required Action |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **$\text{PSI} < 0.10$** | **GREEN** | No significant shift. The model is stable and operating on intended population. Continue routine quarterly surveillance. |
| **$0.10 \le \text{PSI} < 0.25$** | **AMBER** | Moderate population drift. Conduct Characteristic Stability Index (CSI) analysis to identify root-cause features. Prepare for potential recalibration. |
| **$\text{PSI} \ge 0.25$** | **RED** | Significant population shift. Model scores no longer reflect underlying population dynamics. Escalate to Model Risk Committee, initiate Post-Model Adjustment (PMA) and rebuild plan. |

---

#### 3.2 Characteristic Stability Index (CSI)

When a model triggers an Amber or Red PSI breach, the analyst must determine **why** the score shifted. The **Characteristic Stability Index (CSI)** applies the identical PSI formula to individual input features:

$$\text{CSI}_j = \sum_{b=1}^{B_j} (A_{j, b} - E_{j, b}) \times \ln\left(\frac{A_{j, b}}{E_{j, b}}\right)$$

where $B_j$ is the number of bins for input feature $j$.

##### Root-Cause Diagnostics Table in Barclays QA:
| Feature Inspected | Observed CSI | Root Cause Identified in Banking Portfolio |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Bureau Score** | $0.28$ (High) | Change in credit bureau version (e.g., CIBIL TransUnion v2 $\rightarrow$ v3) or loosening of underwriting marketing filters. |
| **Debt-to-Income (DTI)** | $0.19$ (Moderate) | Inflationary pressures causing living expense inflation, or shift toward higher-borrowing segments. |
| **Inquiries in Last 6M** | $0.32$ (High) | Macroeconomic credit tightening causing borrowers to shop around at multiple digital lenders simultaneously. |
| **Employment Duration** | $0.03$ (Low) | Stable demographic characteristic; not contributing to score drift. |

$$\text{KS} = \max_k |F_{\text{bad}, k} - F_{\text{good}, k}| = |80.00\% - 27.92\%| = \mathbf{52.08\%} \quad (\text{located at Decile 3})$$

$$\text{AUC} = \sum_{k=1}^{10} \frac{(F_{\text{bad}, k} + F_{\text{bad}, k-1})}{2} \times (F_{\text{good}, k} - F_{\text{good}, k-1})$$
Evaluating the trapezoidal sum yields:
$$\text{AUC} = 0.8425$$

##### Calculation of Gini Coefficient:
$$\text{Gini} = 2 \times \text{AUC} - 1 = 2 \times 0.8425 - 1 = \mathbf{0.685} \quad (\mathbf{68.5\%})$$
Evaluation: Outstanding discriminatory power for retail unsecured lending.
