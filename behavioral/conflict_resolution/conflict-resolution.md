# Conflict Resolution

## Why Interviewers Ask

Conflict questions evaluate emotional intelligence, communication skills, and whether you protect team health while still advancing work. Interviewers want to see that you address conflict early, separate people from problems, and leave relationships stronger.

## Framework: Listen → Collaborate → Resolve → Learn

1. **Listen:** Gather each side's perspective without interrupting.
2. **Collaborate:** Identify shared goals and surface the real issue (often it is not the stated one).
3. **Resolve:** Agree on a concrete, fair next step and assign owners.
4. **Learn:** Document the lesson and adjust process or communication norms to prevent recurrence.

## Example Prompts and Approach

**Prompt:** Tell me about a situation when you had a conflict with a teammate.

- Situation: Two teammates proposed different approaches for a critical feature; neither would yield.
- Task: Resolve the disagreement while preserving the relationship and project timeline.
- Action: Met separately to understand priorities, then facilitated a team meeting to compare approaches against objective criteria (performance, maintainability, timeline). Proposed prototyping the stronger elements of both.
- Result: A hybrid solution was adopted; the feature shipped on time; both teammates felt heard.

**Prompt:** Describe a time when you had to give someone difficult feedback. How did you handle it?

- Situation: A peer's code lacked documentation, risking long-term maintenance.
- Task: Deliver the feedback without damaging confidence or the working relationship.
- Action: Scheduled a private conversation, started with genuine strengths, explained why documentation matters for the team, and offered examples and a shared style guide.
- Result: Documentation quality improved over the next two sprints; the peer thanked you for the clarity.

**Prompt:** Describe a time when there was a conflict within your team. How did you help resolve it?

- Situation: Two senior members clashed over architecture choice, causing morale to drop.
- Task: Restore focus and trust without taking sides.
- Action: Acknowledged the merits of both views, proposed a time-boxed proof-of-concept for each, and set a neutral evaluation rubric.
- Result: The data favored one approach; the other member accepted the outcome; the team instituted a lightweight RFC process for future decisions.

## Do's and Don'ts

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Take ownership of your part in the conflict | Portray yourself as entirely innocent |
| Focus on behaviors and impacts, not personalities | Use inflammatory language or labels |
| Show that the relationship improved or a process improved | Claim you "never" have conflicts |
| Explain the follow-up prevention step | Leave the answer open-ended |

## Questions to Prepare

- Tell me about a time you had a disagreement with your manager. How did you handle it?
- Describe a situation where you saw a problem and took initiative rather than waiting.
- Give an example of a time when you had to communicate a difficult message.
- What do you do when you receive tough or critical feedback?
- Tell me about a time when you had to adapt to a rapidly changing project requirement.

## Preventive Habits to Mention

- Regular one-on-ones to surface concerns early.
- Written agreements on scope, owners, and deadlines.
- Retrospectives or post-mortems after contentious milestones.
- Clear escalation paths so friction does not fester.
