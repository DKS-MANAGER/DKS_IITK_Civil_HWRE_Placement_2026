### Questions 46–50: Reading Comprehension (Epigenetics & Transcriptional Regulation)

**Passage:**
Classical Mendelian genetics posits that phenotypic traits and cellular heredity are governed strictly by the linear sequence of four nucleotide base pairs comprising the genomic DNA code. However, the paradigm of modern epigenetics has demonstrated that gene expression is fundamentally orchestrated through reversible biochemical modifications of chromatin architecture that do not alter the underlying nucleotide sequence. Chief among these regulatory mechanisms are post-translational histone modifications and covalent cytosine methylation at CpG dinucleotide islands.

In mammalian cells, DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs) catalyze the transfer of a methyl group to the fifth carbon of cytosine residues, generating 5-methylcytosine ($5\text{mC}$). Dense hypermethylation of gene promoter regions typically induces steric hindrance and recruits methyl-CpG-binding domain (MBD) proteins, which in turn assemble histone deacetylase (HDAC) complexes. This enzymatic cascade condenses euchromatin (the transcriptionally active, open chromatin state) into dense heterochromatin, physically occluding transcription factors and silencing downstream gene transcription. Conversely, histone acetyltransferases (HATs) neutralize the positive charge on histone lysine tails, loosening electrostatic bonds with negatively charged DNA phosphate backbones and facilitating active RNA polymerase elongation.

Dysregulation of this epigenetic landscape is now recognized as a primary driver of oncogenesis, neurodegeneration, and cellular senescence. Unlike genetic mutations, which create permanent physical lesions within the DNA sequence, epigenetic modifications are inherently plastic and reversible. This reversibility has fueled intense pharmaceutical development of "epidrugs"—such as HDAC inhibitors and hypomethylating agents like azacitidine. Furthermore, recent breakthroughs in dCas9-fused epigenetic editors permit site-specific methylation deposition or demethylation at designated loci without causing cytotoxic double-stranded DNA breaks, paving the way for programmable synthetic gene therapeutics.
