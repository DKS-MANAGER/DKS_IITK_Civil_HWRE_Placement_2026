### Questions 46–50: Reading Comprehension (Quantum Supremacy & Post-Quantum Cryptography)

**Passage:**
The transition from classical Turing computational paradigms to quantum information processing represents a fundamental architectural rupture in modern computing. Operating on quantum bits (qubits) governed by the principles of linear superposition and quantum entanglement, quantum processors explore exponentially vast Hilbert spaces simultaneously. While classical computers solve intractable mathematical problems in polynomial or exponential time complexity classes ($P$ and $NP$), Shor’s algorithm demonstrates that a fault-tolerant quantum computer can factor large composite integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time.

This algorithmic reality poses an existential threat to modern cryptographic infrastructure. Contemporary public-key cryptosystems—including RSA, Diffie-Hellman, and Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)—derive their cryptographic hardness from the computational infeasibility of integer factorization and discrete logarithms on classical Von Neumann architectures. A sufficiently scaled quantum computer operating several thousand logical error-corrected qubits could compromise asymmetric encryption protocols globally, decrypting legacy financial ledgers, national security communications, and distributed blockchain consensus networks.

In response, the international cryptographic community, led by the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), has initiated the transition toward Post-Quantum Cryptography (PQC). Unlike quantum key distribution (QKD), which requires specialized quantum physical hardware links, PQC relies on mathematical problem classes believed to be secure against both classical and quantum adversaries. Prominent among these are lattice-based cryptography, multivariate polynomial equations, code-based cryptography, and supersingular elliptic curve isogenies. Lattice-based constructions, such as the Learning With Errors (LWE) and Short Integer Solution (SIS) problems, offer compact key sizes and robust worst-case-to-average-case hardness reductions. However, implementing PQC algorithms within legacy enterprise systems presents substantial engineering friction, requiring expanded signature sizes, heightened memory buffers, and recalibrated hardware security modules (HSMs).
